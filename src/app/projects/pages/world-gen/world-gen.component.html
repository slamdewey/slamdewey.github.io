Overview

This is an interactive walkthrough of a procedural world generation system that uses weather simulation techniques to produce realistic biome distributions. The system builds a world through sequential stages: terrain generation, climate modeling, and biome classification.

Each stage below shows a visualization of the generated data. Adjust the parameters and click Regenerate to see how they affect the output.

Stage 1: Tectonic Plates

The generation begins by creating a dense Voronoi tessellation of hundreds of small cells, then grouping them into tectonic plates. This gives plates natural, irregular boundaries rather than smooth geometric edges. Plate sizes follow a power-law distribution — a handful of major plates cover most of the globe while many smaller plates fill the margins, mimicking Earth's actual plate size distribution.

Seed placement uses a two-phase approach: major plates are placed first via farthest-point sampling for even spacing, then a preview growth pass partitions the map to find boundary regions. Minor plate seeds are placed exclusively at these boundaries, ensuring small plates always form at the margins between large plates — never as isolated islands inside them. Plates grow outward from their seeds using a cost-scaled Dijkstra algorithm where each plate's per-hop cost is inversely proportional to its target area, so large plates expand further while small plates stay compact.

Each plate is assigned a type (continental or oceanic) and a random drift vector. Plate boundaries are classified by relative motion: convergent (collision), divergent (spreading), or transform (sliding). The boundary type and plate types determine where mountains, rifts, and fault lines form.

Stage 1b: Plate Interactions

The combination of boundary motion (convergent, divergent, transform) and plate types (continental, oceanic) determines the physical interaction at each boundary. These six interactions each produce a distinct elevation profile — and critically, some are asymmetric: at a subduction zone, the continental side gets mountains while the oceanic side gets a trench.

Each pixel near a boundary gets an elevation delta based on its interaction type, the boundary intensity (how fast the plates are moving relative to each other), and a distance falloff from the boundary. The key insight is that the pixel's own plate type determines which side of an asymmetric interaction it's on:

A Chamfer distance transform computes each pixel's distance to the nearest boundary, and a BFS propagation carries the boundary identity outward. The final base elevation for each pixel is its plate-type base (continental = high, oceanic = low) plus the interaction-specific delta, producing trenches, mountain ranges, rift valleys, and ridges in physically correct locations.

A short Jacobi smoothing pass then relaxes the interior of each plate toward its isostatic baseline. To keep this from ringing passive margins as one-pixel cliffs, the smoothing step is interaction-aware: orogenic boundaries (collision, subduction, oceanic convergence) stay hard-pinned so mountain crests keep their height, while transforms and continental rifts are blended halfway with their neighbors so the seam dissolves into the surrounding terrain.

Stage 2: Elevation

Elevation is the tectonic base plus fBm detail, but the naive version of that pipeline produces polygonal coastlines that trace Voronoi plate edges — because every pixel in a plate inherits the same isostatic base, every percentile-driven sea-level cutoff lands exactly on the plate graph. Three additional mechanisms fix that:

  • Domain-warped sampling. Rather than reading its own pixel's tectonic base, each cell reads the base field at a noise-offset position (amplitude ≈ a quarter of a plate radius, independent seed). Straight Voronoi edges become dendritic — peninsulas, estuaries, barrier islands.
  • Continental sub-relief. A ridged-fBm perturbation is added inside continental plates so interiors aren't uniformly high. This produces cratons, intracontinental swells, and the Hudson-Bay-style drowned basins you see after the percentile cutoff.
  • Ocean age-from-ridge gradient. Oceanic pixels add +0.15 · exp(-d / scale) - 0.10, where d is distance to the nearest divergent boundary. Ridges lift toward the surface, abyssal plains sink, and the seafloor stops looking uniformly flat.

On top of all that, the elevation stage layers fBm noise for terrain detail, normalizes to [-1, 1], and sets sea level via the water-percentage percentile. Because the base is no longer uniform-by-plate, the cutoff now traces natural, irregular coastlines instead of the plate graph.

Stage 2b: Hydrology — Pass 1 (Erosion)

Hydrology runs in two passes. Pass 1 carves drainage into the raw elevation using uniform rainfall; pass 2 (after climate) reuses the same machinery weighted by real precipitation to produce the final rivers and lakes. Splitting it this way lets erosion finish before climate starts — deserts behind ranges end up more pronounced because the orographic sim sees sharpened mountain spines.

The fill step is a priority-flood (Barnes–Lehman 2014). It seeds every ocean cell and Y-edge cell as an outlet at its own elevation, then grows a depression-free “filled” surface inward via a min-heap. Cells where the filled surface sits above the raw elevation become lake pixels, catching the interior endorheic basins you see in the middle of large continents.

On the filled surface, every land cell gets a D8 steepest-descent flow direction. Cells are sorted by descending elevation and walked in order, pushing one unit of flow into each downstream neighbor — this gives a dendritic flow accumulation field where trunk rivers light up near the coast and tributaries feather out into the highlands.

Erosion uses the classic stream-power law: dE = -K · Am · Sn, where A is flow accumulation and S is slope to the downstream neighbor. A few iterations of this, interleaved with a mild hillslope diffusion pass, break the “blobby” noise terrain into sharper ridges and carved valleys. Because m ≈ 0.5, the erosion topology is mostly insensitive to rainfall magnitude — that's what makes the uniform-rainfall assumption defensible for pass 1.

Stage 3: Wind

Wind isn't assigned per-latitude from a lookup table — it falls out of a pressure field built from Earth's zonal-mean atmospheric circulation. Lows sit at the ITCZ along the equator and along the subpolar fronts at ≈60°, while highs sit at the horse latitudes near ≈30° and at the polar caps. This arrangement is what creates the three circulation cells per hemisphere: the Hadley, Ferrel, and polar cells.

Geostrophic balance. Away from the equator, surface air flows along pressure contours rather than straight down the gradient: the Coriolis force deflects moving air to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern hemisphere, and geostrophic wind is what you get when those two forces balance. The strength of the deflection is set by the Coriolis parameter f, which vanishes at the equator. Near the equator our model smoothly blends toward pure down-gradient flow, which is exactly why the ITCZ shows up as a convergence zone rather than a zonal jet.

Surface friction. Real surface winds don't blow perfectly along isobars — friction slows them and a bit of the pressure-gradient force wins, pushing them across the isobars toward the low at roughly 25°. This is the Ekman spiral effect, and it's what turns pure zonal westerlies into the familiar mid-latitude southwesterlies, and what tilts the trade winds into their ENE→WSW orientation rather than a pure easterly.

Seasonal contrast. Continents heat faster than oceans in summer and cool faster in winter, so the pressure field responds: summer continents develop a relative low (air rushes onshore from the warm ocean), while winter continents develop a relative high (air spills offshore from the cold interior). Running the pressure model twice with the correct seasonal temperature field produces a full monsoon reversal between summer and winter winds — the mechanism behind the seasonal precipitation patterns in Stage 5. The named surface bands of each cell emerge directly: trade winds, mid-latitude westerlies, and polar easterlies.

Terrain deflection. A final pass deflects wind around steep terrain. The elevation gradient at each land cell tells us the uphill direction; if the wind has a component pointing uphill, that component is dampened proportional to the slope. The tangential (along-contour) component is preserved and magnitude slows into a range rather than snapping back to unit length, so flow routes around ridges and weakens on windward faces — both behaviors feed Stage 5's orographic lift and rain-shadow terms.

Stage 3b: Ocean Currents

Surface ocean currents are driven by wind, so the wind field over ocean cells doubles as a first-order current field. What matters for climate is whether each current is carrying water from a hotter or colder latitude than the cell it's passing through.

The modifier reduces to -currentY × polewardness × strength, where polewardness is +1 at the north pole and -1 at the south pole. A southward current in the northern hemisphere is advecting cold polar water and yields a negative (cooling) modifier; a northward current in the same hemisphere advects warm equatorial water and warms the cells it passes through. The southern hemisphere flips the sign automatically. A chamfer pass then carries the modifier into the nearest land cells with a linear falloff, so the temperature stage can use it for both ocean and coastal land.

Because the Stage 3 wind field now carries real magnitude (not unit vectors), the current modifier scales naturally with wind strength — weak doldrum winds drive weaker currents, strong trades drive stronger ones. The modifier is clamped to its original range so the downstream temperature math stays well-conditioned.

Stage 4: Temperature (Seasonal)

Temperature is driven by latitude using an inverse parabola T(y) = 1 - (2y - 1)2 — maximum warmth at the equator, minimum at the poles. A small noise jitter adds local variation. Above sea level, an atmospheric lapse rate reduces temperature with elevation.

Coastal moderation blends inland temperatures toward the local ocean baseline using a chamfer distance-to-ocean field, so coasts have smaller swings than continental interiors. The ocean baseline itself is offset by the wind-driven heat advection from Stage 3b — a current carrying water away from the equator warms the cells it passes through (Gulf Stream), while one carrying water toward the equator cools them (California current).

This stage runs twice: once for summer and once for winter. A hemispheric tilt term +t · (-latNorm) warms one hemisphere and cools the other, mirroring the axial tilt that drives Earth's seasons. The mean field used by the visualization here is just (Tsummer + Twinter) / 2; downstream climate stages use the seasonal pair directly so the Köppen classifier can distinguish coldest-month from warmest-month conditions.

Stage 5: Precipitation

Precipitation is computed in a single pass per season as a streamline back-trace: from each land cell, walk against the wind until the trace either reaches an ocean (the moisture source) or runs out of steps. The cell's annual rainfall is then a closed-form combination of the trace's properties — no cloud iteration, no diffusion, no per-cycle averaging.

The full per-cell formula:

precip = latitudeBaseline × oceanReach × orographic × rainShadow × tempBias

Latitude baseline. Encodes Earth's three wet/dry bands per hemisphere from atmospheric circulation: the ITCZ wet equator, the subtropical-high dry bands at ≈30° N/S, the mid-latitude westerly wet bands at ≈50°, and the dry polar caps. The ITCZ shifts toward the warm hemisphere each season (controlled by the ITCZ Shift slider). In monsoon-prone latitudes the dominant seasonal signal actually comes from Stage 3's seasonal wind reversal — onshore flow from a warm summer ocean vs. offshore flow from a cold winter continent — which this streamline trace picks up through the season-specific wind field it walks.

Ocean reach. Moisture flux through a cell decays exponentially with overland distance from the source — oceanReach = exp(-landDistance / decayLength). This is what makes coastlines wet and continental interiors dry, with the right kind of smooth falloff. Cells that never reached an ocean along their trace get a small residual.

Orographic lift and rain shadow. The trace remembers the maximum elevation it crossed. If the cell is higher than its immediate upwind neighbor, it gets an orographic boost (windward face of a mountain). If the trace had to climb over taller terrain to reach the cell, it gets a rain shadow penalty (lee side — the Foehn effect). Together they carve the asymmetric wet/dry bands seen behind real ranges (the Atacama, the Gobi, the Great Basin).

Temperature bias. A mild factor — warm cells nudge precipitation up because warm oceans evaporate more upstream and warm air carries more moisture (Clausius–Clapeyron). The dominant temperature effect on the climate is structural: it lives in where the latitude baseline puts the wet bands, not in a per-cell capacity model.

The streamline runs once for summer and once for winter (different temperature fields, different ITCZ offsets) so the seasonality and aridity stages have a real seasonal pair to work with. The visualization below shows the annual mean (Psummer + Pwinter) / 2.

Stage 5b: Climate Derivatives

With seasonal temperature and precipitation in hand, four derived fields summarize the climate at each cell. They're cheap to compute, but each one captures a distinction that flat moisture and temperature alone can't:

Potential evapotranspiration (PET). The upper bound on water that could leave a cell as vapor given its temperature, regardless of supply. A Thornthwaite-style monotonic curve maps temperature above the frost threshold to PET; below freezing, PET is essentially zero.

Aridity index (UNEP). AI = MAP / PET. Captures the interaction between rainfall and evaporative demand — a polar cell with low rainfall isn't arid because PET is also low. Standard cutoffs: AI < 0.05 hyper-arid, < 0.2 arid, < 0.5 semi-arid, < 0.65 dry sub-humid, otherwise humid. The Köppen B (arid) classification is driven directly off this index.

Seasonality. |Psummer - Pwinter| / MAP. 0 means precipitation is uniform across the year, 1 means everything falls in one season — the difference between an oceanic climate (Cfb) and a monsoon climate (Cwa) at the same total rainfall. The Köppen s / w / f sub-letters key off this metric.

Growing season. Fraction of the year above both the frost threshold and the aridity wilt point. With two seasons it's discrete (0, 0.5, or 1), but it's the right input for distinguishing biomes that hinge on growing-season length: tundra (low growing season) vs. boreal forest, or grassland vs. true desert.

Stage 5c: Köppen Classification

The Köppen–Geiger climate classification assigns each cell to one of ~25 climate classes (Af/Am/Aw, BWh/BWk/BSh/BSk, Cfa/Cfb/Csa/Csb/Cwa/Cwb, Dfa/Dfb/Dfc/Dwa/Dwb/Dsa/Dsb, ET/EF) based on monthly temperature and precipitation thresholds. We adapt those thresholds to our normalized [0, 1] fields so the classifier slots cleanly between aridity and biomes.

The five top-level groups follow a fixed precedence: polar (E) overrides arid (B) only if the warmest month is too cold; otherwise arid wins; then tropical (A) for cells without cold winters; then continental (D) for cells with cold winters; otherwise temperate (C). Sub-letters split each group by warmth tier (a/b/c) and seasonality (s = summer-dry, w = winter-dry, f = no dry season).

The Köppen layer below uses the standard high-saturation Köppen palette so individual classes are readable. The Biomes layer at the bottom of the page renders the same data with a satellite-natural palette that smoothly blends class boundaries with elevation, snow cover, and ocean depth.

Stage 5d: Hydrology — Pass 2 (Rain-Weighted Rivers)

Pass 1 (Stage 2b) carved the terrain with uniform rainfall to lock in topology. Pass 2 reuses the same priority-flood + D8 + accumulation pipeline on the post-erosion surface, but seeds each land cell with the actual Pannual from the climate stage instead of 1.0. Erosion is not rerun — topology stays put.

The result is a flow accumulation field that genuinely reflects rainfall: rivers that should be major (Amazon-style monsoon belts) light up, while rivers that should thin out (deserts, leeward sides of ranges) shrink to nothing. Lake masks come from the same fill step. Pass 2 costs roughly 1 / (erosionIterations + 1) of pass 1 since the inner stream-power loop is skipped, so it adds <10% to total generation time.

Stage 6: Biomes — Satellite View

The final layer is a global-scale rendering rather than a categorical map. Each cell starts from a satellite-natural color keyed off its Köppen class — deep greens for tropical rainforest, paler greens for oceanic and humid subtropical, olive for Mediterranean and savanna, tan for hot deserts, gray-tan for cold deserts, drab khaki for steppe, and pale olive-gray for tundra. The palette is desaturated relative to the high-contrast Köppen layer above so it reads more like Blue Marble than a political map.

On top of the base color, several rendering passes layer in the planetary detail you'd see from orbit:

  • Ocean depth — shallow shelf cells lighten toward cyan, deep basins darken toward navy, giving continental shelves visible relief.
  • Bare-rock blend on terrain above the mountain threshold — high elevations gradually wash out the Köppen color toward stone gray, so the spine of a major range doesn't stay forest-green.
  • Snow caps driven by the seasonal mean temperature and elevation — cold cells get snow regardless of altitude (polar ice), and high cells get snow regardless of latitude (alpine peaks).
  • Rivers and lakes from pass-2 hydrology — rivers blend over the underlying terrain color rather than overwriting it, so they trace through the landscape instead of looking painted on.
  • Hillshading from a top-left light source darkens lee slopes and brightens windward faces, picking up the topography carved by erosion.

The Köppen layer remains the place to inspect raw climate classification; this layer is the synthesis of every previous stage, and it's the one to compare against Blue Marble imagery to gut-check the simulation.
