Overview

This is an interactive walkthrough of a procedural world generation system that uses weather simulation techniques to produce realistic biome distributions. The system builds a world through sequential stages: terrain generation, climate modeling, and biome classification.

Each stage below shows a visualization of the generated data. Adjust the parameters and click Regenerate to see how they affect the output.

Stage 1: Tectonic Plates

The generation begins by creating a dense Voronoi tessellation of hundreds of small cells, then grouping them into tectonic plates. This gives plates natural, irregular boundaries rather than smooth geometric edges. Plate sizes follow a power-law distribution — a handful of major plates cover most of the globe while many smaller plates fill the margins, mimicking Earth's actual plate size distribution.

Seed placement uses a two-phase approach: major plates are placed first via farthest-point sampling for even spacing, then a preview growth pass partitions the map to find boundary regions. Minor plate seeds are placed exclusively at these boundaries, ensuring small plates always form at the margins between large plates — never as isolated islands inside them. Plates grow outward from their seeds using a cost-scaled Dijkstra algorithm where each plate's per-hop cost is inversely proportional to its target area, so large plates expand further while small plates stay compact.

Each plate is assigned a type (continental or oceanic) and a random drift vector. Plate boundaries are classified by relative motion: convergent (collision), divergent (spreading), or transform (sliding). The boundary type and plate types determine where mountains, rifts, and fault lines form.

Stage 1b: Plate Interactions

The combination of boundary motion (convergent, divergent, transform) and plate types (continental, oceanic) determines the physical interaction at each boundary. These six interactions each produce a distinct elevation profile — and critically, some are asymmetric: at a subduction zone, the continental side gets mountains while the oceanic side gets a trench.

Each pixel near a boundary gets an elevation delta based on its interaction type, the boundary intensity (how fast the plates are moving relative to each other), and a distance falloff from the boundary. The key insight is that the pixel's own plate type determines which side of an asymmetric interaction it's on:

A Chamfer distance transform computes each pixel's distance to the nearest boundary, and a BFS propagation carries the boundary identity outward. The final base elevation for each pixel is its plate-type base (continental = high, oceanic = low) plus the interaction-specific delta, producing trenches, mountain ranges, rift valleys, and ridges in physically correct locations.

Stage 2: Elevation

The tectonic stage produces a base elevation field that already encodes plate type and interaction profiles. The elevation stage simply layers Fractal Brownian Motion (fBm) noise on top for terrain detail — continental surfaces get rugged variation while ocean floors stay relatively smooth. The result is normalized to [-1, 1] and sea level is set by the water percentage percentile cutoff.

Stage 2b: Hydrology & Erosion

Before climate runs, a hydrology pass carves drainage into the raw elevation. A priority-flood fill (Barnes–Lehman 2014) seeds every ocean cell and Y-edge cell as an outlet, then grows a depression-free “filled” surface inward — cells where the filled surface sits above the raw elevation become lake pixels, catching interior basins (the endorheic lakes you see in the middle of large continents).

On the filled surface, every land cell gets a D8 steepest-descent flow direction. Cells are then sorted by descending elevation and walked in order, pushing one unit of flow into each downstream neighbor — this gives a dendritic flow accumulation field where trunk rivers light up near the coast and tributaries feather out into the highlands. The flow field is rainfall-agnostic in this pass: terrain alone carries the drainage pattern.

Erosion uses the classic stream-power law: dE = -K · Am · Sn, where A is flow accumulation and S is slope to the downstream neighbor. A few iterations of this, interleaved with a mild hillslope diffusion pass, break the “blobby” noise terrain into sharper ridges and carved valleys. Because erosion runs before wind, ocean currents, and precipitation, the sharper mountain spines feed directly into the orographic and rain-shadow calculations downstream — deserts behind ranges end up more pronounced for free.

Stage 3: Wind

Global wind patterns are modeled after Earth's atmospheric circulation cells. Six latitudinal zones (three per hemisphere) represent the Hadley, Ferrel, and Polar cells. Each zone has alternating vertical and horizontal dominant wind directions, with smooth interpolation between zones and noise-based jitter for natural variation.

A second pass deflects wind around terrain. The elevation gradient at each land cell tells us the uphill direction; if the wind has a component pointing uphill, that component is dampened proportional to the slope and the vector is renormalized. The tangential (along-contour) component is preserved, so flow routes around ridges instead of charging straight into them.

Stage 3b: Ocean Currents

Surface ocean currents are driven by wind, so the wind field over ocean cells doubles as a first-order current field. What matters for climate is whether each current is carrying water from a hotter or colder latitude than the cell it's passing through.

The modifier reduces to -currentY × polewardness × strength, where polewardness is +1 at the north pole and -1 at the south pole. A southward current in the northern hemisphere is advecting cold polar water and yields a negative (cooling) modifier; a northward current in the same hemisphere advects warm equatorial water and warms the cells it passes through. The southern hemisphere flips the sign automatically. A chamfer pass then carries the modifier into the nearest land cells with a linear falloff, so the temperature stage can use it for both ocean and coastal land.

Stage 4: Temperature

Temperature is primarily driven by latitude using an inverse parabola function T(y) = 1 - (2y - 1)^2, which gives maximum warmth at the equator and minimum at the poles. A small noise jitter adds local variation. Above sea level, an atmospheric lapse rate reduces temperature with elevation (higher = colder).

Coastal moderation blends inland temperatures toward the local ocean baseline using a chamfer distance-to-ocean field, so coasts have smaller swings than continental interiors. The ocean baseline itself is offset by the wind-driven heat advection from Stage 3b — a current carrying water away from the equator warms the cells it passes through (Gulf Stream), while one carrying water toward the equator cools them (California current). The chamfer pass also propagates that modifier into the nearest land cells, so coastal climates inherit the asymmetry.

Stage 5: Precipitation

This is the most complex stage — a multi-cycle weather simulation. Each cycle: ocean cells evaporate moisture into clouds; clouds are pushed by wind (advection); precipitation falls based on temperature (warmer air holds more moisture); excess moisture runs off to lower-elevation neighbors. After several cycles, realistic moisture patterns emerge — wet coastlines and dry interiors.

Mountains modify the cycle in two complementary ways. Where wind is blowing into rising terrain, an orographic boost forces extra precipitation on the windward face. Where wind is descending from a higher upwind cell, an explicit rain-shadow factor suppresses precipitation and re-absorbs a fraction of the remaining cloud water back into vapor — the Foehn effect. Together they carve the asymmetric wet/dry bands seen behind real mountain ranges (the Atacama, the Gobi, the Great Basin).

Stage 6: Biome Classification

Finally, each cell is classified into a biome using a Whittaker-diagram-inspired 6×6 lookup table indexed by moisture and temperature. Water cells are classified by depth (shallow vs. deep ocean), and cells above a high elevation threshold become mountains. The remaining land is mapped to one of nine terrestrial biomes: Arctic, Tundra, Desert, Grassland, Savanna, Boreal Forest, Temperate Forest, or Rainforest.
